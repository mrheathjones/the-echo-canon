# Book 1 — Act 2A: Devon's Persuasive Scene (proof-of-concept draft)

POV: close third, anchored on Lucas.
Position: Act 2A — the loaded gun. Real Nolan (barbed, dimming) immediately before the counterfeit shines. Devon sees Lucas truly (the thing Lucas is starved for), is genuinely right enough that the reader half-agrees, and carries one unsettling tell (no Echo weather; one flicker of buried grief). Sets up the midpoint.

---

Devon Cross stood in the Hale kitchen like he'd always been standing there.

That was the first thing Lucas couldn't get past. Everybody who came to their house got *moved* by it — the noise, Dylan narrating a nature documentary at full volume, the twins bickering, Dad defending the Littles, the dog losing its mind. Everybody bobbed in it like a cork. Devon just stood, easy, hands in his pockets, and the house seemed to lower itself around him, like the room itself took a breath and held it.

Everywhere else had weather. The kitchen ran warm and gold tonight, frantic at the edges where Mom was doing four things at once. Nolan's corner of it had gone — Lucas didn't have the word — *thin.* Pale. Like a shirt left out in the sun for a season.

And around Devon there was nothing at all. Not warm, not cold. A clean, still circle where a person's weather should be. Lucas had never seen anybody with no weather. It should have scared him. Mostly it made him want to step closer, the way you lean toward a quiet room when the party's gotten too loud.

"You ready or what." Nolan swung through with his jacket, cuffed the back of Lucas's head — not hard, the usual toll. "Move, bug. You're in the door."

"I'm not in the door."

"You're always in the door. It's your whole thing." But he said it fast and flat, no spin on it, none of the grin that usually turned the insult into a present — and when Lucas looked up to fire back, Nolan was already looking at something Lucas couldn't see, somewhere past the wall, and the thin pale nothing in his corner pulled a half-inch wider.

"You good?" Lucas said. Quieter. The real question.

"I'm great." Nolan flashed it then, the grin, on cue, like flipping the sign in a shop window from CLOSED to OPEN. "Never better. Tell Gramps I'll call him." And he was out the back, the question left lying on the floor where he'd dropped it.

Then it was just Lucas and the stranger with no weather.

He braced for it. The *hey, little man.* The fake interest, the adult crouch where they put their hands on their knees and asked what grade you were in. Lucas had a face ready.

Devon didn't do any of it. He looked at Lucas for a second — the way you'd actually look at a person — and said, "You watch everybody."

Lucas's ready face slid right off.

"You've been doing it since I walked in." No accusation in it. Like he was reading the weather aloud. "Who's mad, who's faking, who's about to start something. You had your brother's mood read before he got his jacket on." He tipped his head. "Exhausting, right? Seeing all of it, all the time. And then you say something, because how do you not — and everybody decides you're the dramatic one."

Lucas didn't answer. His whole chest had gone still in a way he didn't have a name for either.

"I had that," Devon said. "Growing up. Saw everything, felt everything, said all of it out loud. There's a lot of words people use for a kid like that. Too much. Too sensitive. Problem child. Always the problem." He smiled, and the strange part was that it wasn't a sad smile — it should have been. "Nobody once told me it was a *thing I could do.* Not a thing wrong with me. A thing I could do."

And that landed somewhere Lucas kept locked — the exact spot, like Devon had cut a key for it. Because that was the whole thing, wasn't it. Not broken. A thing he could do. Lucas felt his eyes go hot and hated it.

"Your brother's the same engine," Devon said. "You know that. He's just carried the sticker longer, and it gets heavy after a while." Gentle. Certain. "He's tired, Lucas. That's all you're looking at. He's tired of being the problem."

And there it was — the cold little flag, down under all the warmth. Because Lucas *had* been seeing something in Nolan, and it wasn't tired. Tired came back after you slept. This didn't come back. This was the color leaving. He almost said it. He had it right there in his mouth.

But Devon was looking at him like he was worth looking at, and nobody with Nolan's kind of cool had ever looked at Lucas that way without making him earn it first or pay for it after — and the wanting was so big it reached right past the cold flag and shut his mouth for him. *Maybe that's just what calm looks like from outside,* the wanting said. *Maybe you're the one who can't tell rest from wrong, because you've never once had any.*

"You should come by sometime," Devon said, already drifting toward the door, like it was nothing, like it cost nothing. "Not a whole production. No mentors, no clipboards. Just see the place. Just us." He paused with his hand on the frame. For half a second the house-noise swelled — Dylan, the dog, a cabinet banging shut — and something crossed Devon's clean, still face, there and gone, like a fish moving under ice. Something that might have been missing it. Then it smoothed over, the way water smooths. "It's quiet there," he said, and he said *quiet* the way other people said *home.* "You'd be surprised how much you can hear, once it's quiet."

Then he was gone, after Nolan, into the dark.

Lucas stood in the gold, frantic kitchen and the thin pale corner where his brother used to stand. Through the window he watched the two of them cross the yard — the stranger with no weather, and Nolan, who glanced back once at the lit-up house, at Lucas in the window, with a look Lucas couldn't read from that far. Except that it wasn't *great.* It wasn't *never better.* It was something that had forgotten how to ask for help. Then Nolan turned and fell into step beside Devon, easy, matched, and Lucas's stomach went over a cliff —

because from the back, in the dark, he couldn't tell the two of them apart.

# Book 1 — Act 1: The Store Run (Lucas & Nolan) — proof-of-heart draft

POV: close third, anchored on Lucas, narrator held at 13 (he feels, doesn't analyze).
Purpose: bank the brother warmth *before* the graying drains it; live the dyslexia on the page (not as a bible bullet); show Nolan lovable with a hidden cost (unbought kindness disguised as a barb). No Echo, no verbs, no thesis line — a beat that exists because they're people. Nolan's kindness is practical solidarity (he handles the words so Lucas never has to in public), framed as a conspiracy rather than charity — no claim that Nolan shares the struggle.

---

Nolan drove like the car had personally insulted him — one wrist on the wheel, the other arm out the window, music too loud, taking the corner by the gas station hard enough that the bag of returnables in the back tipped over and Lucas had to catch it with his foot.

"Smooth," Lucas said.

"You're welcome." Nolan didn't look over. "Gramps takes that corner at like four miles an hour. Narrates the whole time, too." He dropped into the Gramps growl, dead-on: "'Now this here's the Hendersons' old place. Used to be a barn there.'" Back to his own voice: "There's a barn everywhere with that man. Whole county used to be barns."

Lucas laughed before he meant to.

It was supposed to be Gramps today. It was always supposed to be Gramps. But Gramps's knee was bad and Mom was at work and Dad was doing the thing with the grill that ate the whole afternoon, so it was Nolan — tossed the keys and a twenty and a Mom-list, and Lucas in the passenger seat, which he'd never once say out loud that he liked.

Mom had texted the list to Lucas, not Nolan, because last time Nolan "forgot" half of it on purpose so he could leave early. So it was Lucas's job, and Lucas had been not-looking at his phone the whole ride, because lists were the worst kind of reading there was. No story to lean on. No guessing the word from the shape of the sentence around it. Just a stack of words standing in a line, waiting to trip him.

Inside, under the big lights, it got worse. He had the list up and the words were doing the thing — sliding, the letters in *bread* and the brand of the sauce Dad liked swapping places when he looked right at them — and there was a lady with a cart waiting behind him in the narrow part of the aisle. Not even impatient. Just there. And his face was getting hot, the way it did, the heat climbing up from his collar where everybody could see it —

"Bro, give it." Nolan plucked the phone out of his hand without breaking stride. "You read like you're getting paid by the hour." He scanned it once, walking. "Eggs, bread, the gross sauce, the *other* gross sauce, Dylan's cereal that's just sugar with a cartoon on it, the water that costs more than the gas to come get it. Cool." He handed the phone back without looking and snagged a cart. "Keep up, slow guy."

And that was the thing about Nolan that Lucas could never explain to anybody, because written down it just looked like Nolan being a jerk — grabbing his phone, calling him slow. But the hot thing in Lucas's face had stopped. Nolan hadn't asked if he was okay. He hadn't done the soft voice. He'd walked straight into it and taken it over and made it about being *fast*, which Lucas wasn't, instead of about *reading*, which Lucas couldn't — and the lady with the cart was already gone, and the aisle was just an aisle again.

Nolan shopped the way he drove. He threw the egg carton in from a distance and called it "ordnance." He argued that the cereal counted as a vegetable because there was a leaf on the box. He grabbed an extra tub of the brisket rub that wasn't on the list, because "Jack texts when there's brisket and I want him to suffer," and Lucas thought about Jack at school getting a picture of a meal he wasn't there for, and how that was somehow the nicest thing any of them did to each other.

In the checkout line Nolan went quiet, which he didn't do. Lucas followed his eyes to the front of the store, where a couple of guys Nolan's age were heading out, laughing about something, shoving each other through the door. Nolan watched them the way you'd watch a bus you maybe should have been on. Then his phone buzzed in his pocket and he looked at it and something crossed his face and went, and he put the phone away without answering and said, "What're you staring at," even though Lucas wasn't the one who'd been staring.

In the car he didn't start it right away. He sat with both hands on the wheel and looked at the store like it owed him money.

"You should tell Mom," he said. "About the reading. If it's getting worse."

"It's not getting worse."

"Okay." He didn't push it, which from Nolan was its own kind of loud. He started the car. Then, pulling out of the lot — like it was the laziest idea in the world and not something he'd clearly already worked out: "New system. You text me the list before we leave the house. I'll have the whole thing memorized before we're out of the driveway. You push the cart and look pretty." He shrugged, eyes on the road. "We tell Mom you got fast. She doesn't need our whole business."

It wasn't help, the way Nolan did it. It was a scam, the two of them against Mom and the list and the lady with the cart, and Lucas was on the inside of it. He didn't say thank you, because they didn't, and Nolan would only have called him soft and wrecked it. So he said, "You're still a terrible driver."

"I'm an *incredible* driver."

He took the corner by the gas station even harder on the way back, on purpose, so the returnables would tip over —

except they'd already done the returnables. So nothing fell. And Nolan looked so genuinely betrayed by this, so personally wronged by an empty back seat, that Lucas was still laughing when they pulled into the driveway and the smoke from Dad's grill rolled across the windshield like weather.

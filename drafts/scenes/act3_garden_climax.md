# Book 1 — Act 3: Garden Climax (proof-of-concept draft)

POV: close third, anchored on Lucas, with one rationed lean into Abby at the moment of choice.
Position: Act 3 finale set-piece (B-plot). The manifestation = Abby's suppressed, unfinished self, pulled to "settle"/harden under the campus Gray. Demonstrates the distributed engine (Eli mechanism, Maya names, Zoe opens her hands, Mason's costly vulnerability) and Option B (naming reveals; Abby's costly choice resolves; resolution = preserved aliveness, not calm).

---

The reflection garden was the quietest place Lucas had ever stood in, and that was the whole problem.

Everywhere else on campus the Echo had weather — gym joy that buzzed in your teeth, the grief-fog pooled outside the counseling wing. Here there was nothing. Flat gray air the color of a channel that didn't exist. Adrian Mercer's carvings sat on every ledge and stone, owls and foxes and a turtle the size of a fist, and they were so still they made the stillness worse, as if they were waiting for everyone else to match them.

In the middle of all that nothing, the thing they'd been chasing flickered.

Lucas caught it first. He always did; it was the one trick his brain ran for free. The flicker was small and too bright for the gray, and it wouldn't hold a shape — fox, then bird, then something with too many edges, then a kid-shaped smear, then fox again, fast and scared, trying on every answer like somebody was about to grade it.

His whole body leaned to go at it. Two days ago he'd decided this thing was a monster, and monsters got handled —

"Lucas." Zoe's hand hit his chest, flat. "No."

Right. Two days ago he'd handled it, and it had screamed in a register only he could hear and burrowed into the dark, and Abby had walked off the field with her face shut like a door. He held still. It hurt, holding still; his feet wanted a vote.

Eli crouched at the edge of the gravel, reading the world the way he read wiring. "It's not running *from* us," he said slowly. "It's running from *them*." He nodded at the carvings. "The stillness comes off the figures. Every time it flickers toward the turtle, it slows down. The garden wants it to pick something and stop."

"Stop being what?" Mason said.

"Stop being all of them. Settle. Harden." Eli swallowed. "Like the wood."

On the nearest ledge a new carving had started, though no one had touched it — rough, half-finished, and the shape rising out of it had Abby's chin.

She saw it too. Catherine had made her come, and she stood with her arms crossed in the confident-Accord-girl posture she wore like a backpack, but her eyes were on that half-carved chin and the posture was failing at the seams.

"It's gross," Abby said. Her voice held. Her hands didn't. "It should just settle down. It's embarrassing." She was looking straight at the flicker on the word, and the flicker stuttered and dimmed as if she'd thrown water on it, and the gray crept up an inch.

That was when Lucas understood he had it wrong again. He'd thought the flickering thing was attacking Abby. It wasn't attacking her. It *was* her — the part that didn't know yet, the part her whole life had told her to finish and put away, until she'd gotten so good at being embarrassed by it that she'd help the garden kill it herself.

He opened his mouth, the whole explanation loaded and fast and certain, because he was always so sure —

and he caught Maya's eye, and shut it.

Maya had said nothing the entire time. She'd been standing very still, doing the read, and now she did the thing she never let herself do: she decided. She stepped past him. She wasn't loud. She never was.

"You're not broken," she said to the flicker. Then, to Abby, quieter: "You're not broken. You're not *finished*. And everybody keeps trying to finish you." She glanced at the chin coming up out of the wood. "It's not the monster. It's the part of you they don't have a slot for."

The flicker pulsed toward her, hopeful, then flinched back toward the turtle, toward stop, toward the easy gray nothing where it wouldn't have to be afraid. Lucas could feel how easy it was from ten feet off — the way it offered to turn the volume down, all the way down — and for one ugly second he thought of Nolan at the dinner table, calm, calm, calm, and how much everyone loved him calm.

"Don't make her grab it," he said to Zoe, who was already shifting her weight into the plan where they cornered it and forced the win. "You can't grab this one. There's nothing to grab."

Zoe hated a problem with no handle more than anything alive. It cost her. Then she let her hands open.

It was Mason who moved — not at the flicker, at Abby. He didn't make it a thing. He scuffed up beside her, bored, like it was nothing. "I don't know what I am either, for the record. Jock. Robot guy. Both's annoying. Carter's got it all figured out and I'm just —" he wobbled his hand, a flicker, "— this. It's fine." It wasn't a speech; Mason couldn't make a speech if you paid him. But Abby looked at him, and something in the door of her face came unlatched.

She looked back at the bright scared thing that was hers.

And here — for one breath — the scene was no longer Lucas's to narrate, because the only person who could feel what came next was Abby, standing in a garden built so that nobody would ever have to admit she didn't know. Every wood figure on every ledge was a kid who'd picked an answer. Her father had picked one for her before she could talk. Uncle Adrian had a slot with her name on it, kind and warm and already carved, and all she had to do to be loved in this place was climb into it and go still. The not-knowing clawed at her like something drowning. She was so tired of being embarrassed by it.

"I don't know what I want to be," Abby said.

In that garden it was the most illegal sentence there was. She said it anyway — small and wrecked and out loud — and she didn't fix it, didn't add *but I'll figure it out*, didn't soften it. She let it sit there, unfinished, embarrassing, alive.

The flicker didn't calm.

That was the part Lucas would keep. It didn't go peaceful — it flared, brighter and wilder, a hundred shapes a second, refusing to pick one, refusing to stop, the loudest most chaotic beautiful thing in that dead gray garden. And the gray pulled back from it like a tide going out. The half-carved chin crumbled to sawdust, and ordinary dusk light came back into the air all at once, and far off the campus had weather again.

Nobody won it. Abby just refused to let them finish her, and the refusing was the whole rescue.

Lucas didn't say any of that out loud. For once.

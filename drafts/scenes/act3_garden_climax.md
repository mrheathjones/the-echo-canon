The reflection garden was the quietest place Lucas had ever stood in, and that was the whole problem.

Everywhere else on campus the Echo had weather — gym joy that buzzed in your teeth, the grief-fog pooled outside the counseling wing. Here there was nothing. Flat gray air the color of a channel that didn't exist. Adrian Mercer's carvings sat on every ledge and stone, owls and foxes and a turtle the size of a fist, and they were so still they made the stillness worse, as if they were waiting for everyone else to match them.

In the middle of all that nothing, the thing they'd been chasing flickered.

Lucas caught it first. He always did; it was the one trick his brain ran for free. The flicker was small and too bright for the gray, and it wouldn't hold a shape — fox, then bird, then something with too many edges, then a kid-shaped smear, then fox again, fast and scared, trying on every answer like somebody was about to grade it.

His whole body leaned to go at it. Two days ago he'd decided this thing was a monster, and monsters got handled —

"Lucas." Zoe's hand hit his chest, flat. "No."

Right. Two days ago he'd handled it, and it had screamed in a register only he could hear and burrowed into the dark, and Abby had walked off the field with her face shut like a door. He held still. It hurt, holding still; his feet wanted a vote.

Eli was already crouched at the edge of the gravel, reading the world the way he read wiring, and Lucas could see him liking the shape of it, the way Eli always got calmer the worse a thing got. "It's not running from us," he said. "It's running from them." He nodded at the carvings. "The stillness comes off the figures. Field effect. Every time it flickers toward the turtle, it slows. It's a feedback loop, and a loop has a source." He stood, eyes going to the nearest ledge, where a new carving had started though no one had touched it — rough, half-finished, the shape rising out of it already wearing Abby's chin. "There. That one's the anchor. Knock it off the ledge, break the field, and the thing comes loose." He was moving before he finished saying it, sure as a closing circuit, his hand already going for the wood —

"Eli — *don't* —"

It was Maya and Lucas at the same time, but it was Mason who got there with his body, an arm slung across Eli's chest the same way Zoe's had just hit his, and for a second the two of them were tangled and stumbling on the gravel, Eli twisting to finish the reach he was so certain of — and the flicker *shrieked,* that under-hearing register, and it didn't flinch away from the breaking carving. It lunged *toward* it. Frantic. Like Mason was about to drop a kid off a roof. The gray crept up an inch while everyone was looking at the wrong thing.

"It's not wiring." Maya's voice cut clean across all of it, and Eli stopped like he'd hit glass. She wasn't loud. She never was. But she'd done the thing she never let herself do in time to matter — decided, out loud, while it still counted. "It's not a circuit, Eli. There's no source to break. You'd just be breaking *her.*"

Eli's hand hung an inch off the wood. He wasn't moving. Lucas had never seen him not know what to do next, and it was worse to watch than anything the flicker did. Then he pulled his hand back like the carving had gone hot.

Mason let go of him. Set himself between Eli and the ledge anyway, just in case, breathing hard.

And then there was the no-handle of it, and Lucas felt the whole group hit it at once. There was nothing to *do.* Zoe felt it worst. Her weight was already dropping into a plan — flank it, pin it, force the win, the way her dad would, *slow progress is better than no progress, play the cards you're dealt* — her hands coming up to fold the team into a shape that moved.

And then she didn't.

She made herself look at the bright scared thing instead of at the problem, really look, and Lucas saw the exact second she understood there was no play here — nothing to flank, nothing to pin, nothing to drive at. "You can't grab this one," she said, low, almost to herself. Her jaw worked. "There's nothing to grab. It's not ours to —" She stopped. Her hands came down, open and useless at her sides, and that was harder for Zoe than running into anything would have been. He could see how much she hated it — being the one who, this time, couldn't.

"It's gross," Abby said, fast, from where she'd been standing apart the whole time. Arms crossed in the confident-Accord-girl posture she wore like a backpack, eyes pinned to that half-carved chin, the posture failing at the seams. "It should just settle down. It's embarrassing." She was looking straight at the flicker on the word, and the flicker stuttered and dimmed as if she'd thrown water on it, and the gray crept up another inch.

The flicker pulsed toward Maya, hopeful, then flinched back toward the turtle, toward *stop,* toward the easy gray nothing where it wouldn't have to be afraid. Lucas could feel how easy it was from ten feet off — the way it offered to turn the volume all the way down — and for one ugly second he thought of Nolan at the dinner table, calm, calm, calm, and how much everyone loved him calm.

The explanation loaded in him then, the whole thing, fast and certain, ready to spill — *it's her, it's the part of her that doesn't know yet, it's not attacking her, it IS her* — and he was always so sure.

He caught Maya's eye. He shut his mouth.

She turned to Abby and committed. "You're not broken," she said. Then, quieter, landing it: "You're not broken. You're not *finished.* And everybody keeps trying to finish you." She glanced at the chin coming up out of the wood, the half-carved jaw that wasn't a monster's, and let it sit there.

It was Mason who moved then — not at the flicker, at Abby. He didn't make it a thing. He scuffed up beside her, hands jammed in his pockets, bored, like it was nothing, like the carving hadn't almost cracked open between him and Eli a minute ago. "I don't know what I am either, for the record." He shrugged at the ground. "Jock. Robot guy. Both's annoying. Carter's got it all figured out and I'm just —" he wobbled his hand, a flicker, "— this. It's fine." It wasn't a speech; Mason couldn't make a speech if you paid him. But Abby looked at him, and something in the door of her face came unlatched.

She looked back at the bright scared thing that was hers.

And here — for one breath — the scene was no longer Lucas's to narrate, because the only person who could feel what came next was Abby, standing in a garden built so that nobody would ever have to admit she didn't know.

Every wood figure on every ledge was a kid who'd already picked an answer. Her father had picked hers before she could talk — Nathan, the Curator, who never once raised his voice, who only ever needed her to not embarrass the movement, to stand up straight at the front of the room and be the proof that the Accord made you whole, and whose face went easy and warm only when she carried it right. She was so hungry for the easy version of his face. And Uncle Adrian, who'd been as close as she had to a second parent — since the mother she actually had was a name and a photograph and a grave she'd been too young to remember being filled — Adrian had sent her here, gentle as he was about everything, *go sit in the garden, Abs, let it quiet you,* and somewhere on his workbench he kept a slot with her name already carved in it, warm and patient and waiting, *I knew what was in there the moment I met you.* All she had to do to keep him looking at her like that was climb in and go still. This was the only home there was. There was nothing on the other side of *not* belonging here — no second family in a backup drawer, no mother to run to. The settled girl got all of it. The settled girl got the warm face, the carved slot, the front of the room.

The not-knowing clawed at her like something drowning. She was so tired of being embarrassed by it. She knew exactly what it would cost.

"I don't know what I want to be," Abby said.

In that garden it was the most illegal sentence there was. She said it anyway — small and wrecked and out loud — and she didn't fix it, didn't add *but I'll figure it out,* didn't soften it for her father or for Adrian or for the slot. She felt every one of them go out of reach as she said it. She let it sit there, unfinished, embarrassing, alive.

The flicker didn't calm.

That was the part Lucas would keep. It didn't go peaceful — it flared, brighter and wilder, a hundred shapes a second, refusing to pick one, refusing to stop, the loudest most chaotic beautiful thing in that dead gray garden. And the gray pulled back from it like a tide going out. The half-carved chin crumbled to sawdust off the ledge, and ordinary dusk light came back into the air all at once, and far off the campus had weather again.

Nobody won it. Abby just refused to let them finish her, and the refusing was the whole rescue.

Lucas didn't say any of that out loud. For once.

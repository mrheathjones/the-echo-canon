Ben Carter had drawn a chalk line down the middle of the room and asked everyone to stand on the side that fit them.

It was supposed to be easy. *Builders* on the left, *finders* on the right — a warm-up, a way to talk about tracks. Ben kept saying that, "warm-up," like if he said it enough times it would stop being a test. He was new at this. You could tell because he laughed before his own jokes landed, and because his clipboard shook a little, and because when nobody moved he looked at the line like it had betrayed him.

Lucas stayed where he was, which was the worst place, the middle, his sneakers half on the chalk. He was used to being the kid who needed re-explaining — the one teachers circled back to, slower the second time. Maya was beside him not choosing either, the way she didn't choose things. Across the room one of the cohort kids kept stepping toward *finders* and stepping back, stepping and stepping back, and the longer it went the redder his ears got, and Ben opened his mouth to help and didn't have the help ready.

Abby crossed the line like it was a doorway she'd walked through her whole life.

"Builders," she said. "Curation, really. Like my dad — finding the thing in someone before they can see it, and protecting it until they can." She smiled at the stalled kid, this kind, easy smile. "It took me forever too," she said, which wasn't true, anybody could see it had never taken her any time at all, but she said it so warmly that the kid laughed in relief and stepped left and stood by her, *settled*, fixed, done — and the red went out of his ears. Ben exhaled like she'd untied something for him. "Right," he said. "Yeah. That's — thank you, Abby."

And he wrote it down. *On the board.* In her words, under one of the turnoffs. They were writing down what *she* said.

A woman Lucas didn't know leaned in the doorway — staff, lanyard, the unhurried way the grown-ups here moved. When Abby talked, the woman's whole face went soft and *settled*, the way you look at a kid who's already turned out fine, nothing left to worry about. "That's Nathan's girl," she said to someone beside her, proud, like it explained everything — and Abby didn't turn, but you could see her catch it, the way a plant turns without seeming to move. Lucas felt it from across the room and felt, underneath it, a fast ugly want: *do that at me. Look at me like the answer's already in.*

To walk into a room and have the room already hold a place for you. And he thought, with a lurch that felt almost like falling: *that could be me. If I'd been here. If I'd grown up knowing where the line was.* He almost stepped onto the *builders* side just to feel it.

On the windowsill behind Ben there was a little carved owl, smooth and brown, one of the dozens scattered all over campus, on every ledge and over every doorframe — Lucas had stopped seeing them the way you stop seeing doorknobs. Abby reached back without looking and rested two fingers on its back, light, the way you'd quiet a dog. Like it had been carved to sit exactly under her hand.

Then — for half a second — something moved in her face. The stalled kid had asked her something, low, *but what if you're not sure which one,* and her answer was already coming, smooth, but underneath the smooth there was a flicker, a flinch she caught and swallowed before it finished, like she'd reached for a word in her own language and come up empty. Her fingers went still on the owl. She laughed instead. "You just know," she said, and the flinch was gone, snuffed so fast Lucas wasn't sure he'd seen it.

But while she said it — *you just know* — while everyone nodded and Ben wrote something grateful on his shaking clipboard, the air around her went the littlest bit gray. Not dark. Not anything you could point at. Just a thinning, a place where the color sat down, the way a room dims when one cloud crosses the sun and nobody looks up. It pooled where she was brightest. Right where everyone was looking.

Lucas blinked and it was ordinary again. He almost said *did anybody else* — and didn't. Probably the light. Probably him.

"She's working so hard at it," Maya said, very quietly, not really to him.

"At what? She's not working at anything." That was the part he envied.

Maya didn't argue. She had the look she got right before she said a true thing, and then — the way she did — she let it go by, kept it for later.

Ben capped his marker. "*That's* what I'm talking about," he said to the whole circle, and he was looking at Abby when he said it. Abby sat in the middle of the warmth like water finding its level, home. Lucas stood on the chalk line and did not step over it, and did not step back either, and stood there in the middle where the floor felt thinnest of all.

## Positioning

- **Format:** illustrated prose hybrid (prose for family/interiority/adult tragedy; full-bleed art for the visual metaphors).
- **Category/age:** upper middle grade; protagonist Lucas is 13.
- **Logline:** A 13-year-old who can see the hidden emotional layer of the world is the only one who notices when his magnetic older brother starts quietly vanishing into a kind, well-meaning institution that calls erasure "healing" — and his certainty that he alone is right nearly costs him the brother he's trying to save.
- **Comps:** *Amari and the Night Brothers*, *Nevermoor*; *A Wrinkle in Time* (Camazotz) as the thematic touchstone for the Gray.
- **Rep:** ADHD **and dyslexia**, both load-bearing; hero is not broken, and is often wrong.

## The world, briefly

The Echo is a hidden emotional layer of reality, objectively real but perceived differently by each mind. Lucas filters less, so he catches more — a burden, not a superpower. **Attunement** is growth available to everyone, dramatized as choices, never spellcasting.

**The magic is perception, not power.** Scenes are won by seeing correctly and acting on it; lost by misreading and acting on the misread — which is also Lucas's core flaw ("being misunderstood doesn't mean you're right"). The verbs: **Catch → Read → Hold → Name → Choose.** Resolution model (**Option B**): naming a manifestation *reveals* what it needs; a *costly choice* — usually by the person it belongs to — *resolves* it. Wins look like preserved aliveness, never calm.

**The Gray** is the institution's flattening, experienced by the grayed as peace. It is **intended-as-mercy**: the Accord sincerely believes quieting is healing. Mechanically, the Gray is the relief taken when someone is named truly and *declines* the costly choice.

**The Accord** is a legitimate, non-cartoonish institution founded from grief (the Nathan/Adrian/Sarah tragedy — kept off-page in Book 1). Its danger is method and endpoint, not malice.

## Cast (Book 1)

The Hale family (loud, intact, loving, imperfect — a deliberate differentiator). **Nolan** (~18), the beloved, manipulative older brother, is Book 1's emotional engine. Core friends **Maya Sinclair, Zoe Bennett, Mason Reed, Eli Sullivan**, recruited into the Accord program with Lucas; each owns a distinct role in solving Echo problems (reader-of-people / driver / anchor / reader-of-systems) so the group, not Lucas alone, does the work. **Abby Whitmore**, the founder's daughter, the insider. **Devon Cross**, a sincere young-adult-track recruiter and the Book 1 antagonistic force — himself an Accord "success story," and a *counterfeit* of the clean older brother Lucas never got.

## Book 1 spine (full beat sheet: `canon/story/BOOK_1_SPINE.md`)

Two plots that are one: **A-plot** — Nolan grays, Lucas alone sees it, his certainty drives a rash "rescue" that makes it worse, and he learns he cannot choose for Nolan, only stop treating him like the problem. **B-plot** — Abby's suppressed, unfinished self manifests and is resolved not by defeating it but by Abby's costly choice. Lucas practices the truth on a near-stranger and graduates by failing to force it on his brother.

## What we want pressure-tested (the second-review questions)

1. **The midpoint hinge.** Does "Lucas is finally *right*, and being right is what ruins him" land as earned and sympathetic, or as the author punishing him?
2. **The ensemble.** Does the distributed friend-group engine read as a living ensemble, or as a mechanical party of assigned roles? Are the four friends now distinct (we de-overlapped their wants), or do they still blur?
3. **Devon / the Accord.** Is Devon persuasive enough that a reader genuinely half-agrees? Does "intended-as-mercy" keep the institution non-cartoonish while still giving the series teeth?
4. **Altitude.** Does the emotional/philosophical altitude sit right for upper MG with a 13-year-old lead, or does it skew older/younger?
5. **The preaching risk.** The bible's deepest rule is "don't preach the theme." Do the scenes show-not-tell, or does a lesson-delivery scent escape?
6. **ADHD/dyslexia rep.** Is the perception-as-burden framing handled with care; does the hero-is-often-wrong move work without shaming?
7. **Series engine / endgame (unresolved — chew on it).** A non-evil institution can't take a final battle. What's the strongest endgame shape: reform, schism, or a faction breaking bad? And what's the "second clock" once the founding grief resolves?
